    Part I · The Debt

    In one sentence
    How much the US owes, what kinds of IOUs make it up, when they come due, and why that decides how fast interest costs move.

    Builds on
    Lesson 3 (borrowing) and lesson 4 (short vs long).

    —

    Three different numbers get called "the debt." Gross debt counts everything Treasury has issued, including the government owes its own trust funds: Social Security, Medicare, federal pensions. That part is one arm of the government owing another.

    Debt held by the public is what's owed to everyone else. "The public" includes the Federal Reserve, which held of Treasuries on Sep 30. Marketable debt is the part that can be traded: the stock that markets price, finance in repo and refinance every week. The rest is non-marketable.

    Congress caps gross debt with the debt limit, currently . On , counted against it, leaving of room.

    What it's made of

    Marketable debt comes in five kinds of security, which differ mainly in how long their interest rate stays fixed. Bills are repaid within a year and reborrowed at whatever rate is current. A 10-year note locks its coupon for a decade. Shading below goes from lightest (rate resets fastest) to darkest (rate fixed longest).

    Bills are of marketable debt, above the 15–20% range Treasury's advisory committee (TBAC) recommends. Leaning on bills is cheaper when short rates are below long rates, and it is what money funds and stablecoin issuers want to buy. The cost is that interest bills respond faster when rates move.

    How it's sold

    All of it is sold at auctions on a public calendar, almost every business day. Every three months the Quarterly Refunding sets the plan. On , Treasury said it expects to borrow from private investors in Oct–Dec 2026 and in Jan–Mar 2027. That's enough to cover the deficit and end those quarters with and in the TGA. The statement on auction sizes is due Nov 4 at 8:30 a.m. ET.

    Primary dealers must bid at every auction, so a sale never fails. Who ends up holding the bonds is the signal people watch.     Refinancing simulator

    Set the rates Treasury pays on new debt and see where the interest bill goes. Each month, maturing securities are replaced. Bills are rolled at the short rate, with about a third of the bill stock repricing each month. Everything else is refinanced into new notes and bonds at the long rate, in Treasury's current auction mix (2 to 30 years). The coupons on existing debt stay fixed until each security matures.

    Is a rising interest bill a problem? Economists disagree. Some focus on whether interest rates (r) stay below the economy's growth rate (g), in which case debt relative to GDP can stabilize. Others stress that interest crowds out other spending, or that it raises the risk of fiscal dominance. This page only shows the mechanics.

    Who owns it

    The government owns a large share of its own debt: trust funds plus the Federal Reserve. Foreigners hold about a quarter, according to Treasury's TIC survey; about 40% of that is central banks and governments. The rest is spread across US money funds, banks, mutual funds, state and local governments, pensions, insurers and households.     Part II · The Plumbing

    In one sentence
    The same accounts as in the lessons, at real size: how dollars move between the Fed, the Treasury, banks, money funds and markets.

    Builds on
    Lessons 1–2 (reserves, TGA), 5–6 (Fed balance sheet, ON RRP) and 7 (repo). New here? Try the scenarios in this order: 2, 1, 3, 4, 7, 6, 5.

    When the government borrows, spends or taxes, the dollars don't appear or vanish. They move between a handful of big accounts, and most of those accounts sit in one place: the Federal Reserve.

    The Fed's balance sheet is the centre of the map. On the left are its assets, mostly Treasuries and mortgage bonds. On the right is what it owes: paper currency, banks' reserves, the Treasury's checking account and overnight deposits from money funds. The two columns are always the same height. That's not a policy, it's accounting.

    The Treasury General Account is the government's checking account at the Fed. When taxes come in or new bonds are sold, it fills up. Because Fed assets don't change, something else on the right has to shrink, usually bank reserves. When the government spends, the money flows back.

    The overnight reverse repo facility used to be a second reservoir: money funds parked up to there in late 2022. It is now essentially empty (). That's what "the RRP is drained" means: new drains now come straight out of bank reserves.

    Below the Fed sits the repo market, a pawn shop for bonds. Money funds lend cash overnight against Treasuries. Dealers pass it on to hedge funds that hold Treasuries with borrowed money. If private cash runs short, the Fed's standing repo lends at the top of its rate corridor.

    Repo trades in three venues. In tri-party repo ( a day on Nov 2), a clearing bank holds the collateral; money funds lend here. In cleared repo (about a day), trades go through a central clearing house (FICC) that nets them. Bilateral repo is negotiated privately, often with no haircut, and isn't in the published numbers. The SEC's clearing mandate moves most cash Treasury trades into clearing by Dec 31, 2026, and most repo by Jun 30, 2027.

    Banks hold of Treasuries alongside their reserves. When rates rose in 2022–23, the unrealized losses on those bonds helped set off the run on Silicon Valley Bank (Mar 10, 2023). The Fed's BTFP then lent against such bonds at face value for a year. The dealer arms of big banks are limited by the supplementary leverage ratio, which counts even Treasuries and reserves. A reform effective Apr 1, 2026 loosened it to give dealers more room in a sell-off.

    Part III · Connections

    In one sentence
    Decodes the phrases in headlines and links the plumbing to interest rates, crypto and the global dollar.

    Builds on
    All eight lessons, especially lesson 8 (the corridor). Each phrase links back to the part of the page that explains it.

    The same few accounts explain most of the headlines. Below are phrases you'll hear, decoded. Each one says whether it describes an accounting fact, a rule of thumb (heuristic), or a narrative that people argue about, and points to the live number behind it.

    Phrases you'll hear

    The rate corridor

    The Fed doesn't set market rates directly. It sets a floor (what it pays money funds at the ON RRP), a ceiling (what it charges at the standing repo) and a rate in between (IORB, paid on bank reserves). Market rates such as SOFR and fed funds trade inside that box. Where they sit shows how scarce cash is. Spikes in the 99th percentile mostly fall on quarter-ends and tax dates. When reserves were plentiful in 2024, SOFR traded near the floor. Now it trades above IORB and close to the ceiling.

    Crypto bridge read with care

    Stablecoins are a new pipe into the Treasury market. Dollars go in, tokens come out, and the issuer parks the dollars mostly in short Treasury bills. Under the GENIUS Act (signed Jul 18, 2025), payment stablecoins must be backed 1:1 by cash, bank deposits, Treasuries maturing within 93 days, short repo on such Treasuries, or government money funds. That makes issuers structural buyers of bills. Scale: about of stablecoins exist (secondary source), against of bills outstanding. Tether alone reports about of bills, roughly of the bill market. That's large for one company, small for the market. Step through a $10B inflow →

    Why bitcoin traders watch Fed plumbing. The popular story: when the Fed's balance sheet grows, or cash leaves the TGA and the ON RRP, more money is left in private hands to bid up risk assets. That is "liquidity" in its loosest sense. Real yields (what you give up by holding something with no yield) and the dollar (global funding conditions) are watched for similar reasons.     The honest caveats

    • Net liquidity is a trader's heuristic, not an official measure. It leaves out bank lending and deposits, money-fund cash, foreign dollars and the rest of the world's central banks.
    • The lines moved together in some periods (2020–21 both up, 2022 both down) and apart in others. From Dec 2022 to Dec 2024, bitcoin rose about while net liquidity barely moved ( → ). Picking the matching periods is easy after the fact.
    • Bitcoin has its own drivers: ETF flows, leverage and liquidations, the halving cycle, regulation, adoption. They often matter more.
    • Correlation is not mechanism. The mechanical links on this page run between Fed liabilities (reserves, TGA, ON RRP). The link from there to any asset price is a market belief, and it can change.

    The global dollar

    Trillions of dollars are borrowed outside the US (eurodollars). In a crisis, foreign banks scramble for dollars, the cross-currency basis widens, and the Fed lends dollars to other central banks through swap lines, which they pass on to their own banks. Swap lines are a Fed asset, so they create reserves just as QE does. Today they are idle: outstanding, against in May 2020 and in Dec 2008.

    Foreign central banks also keep parked overnight at the Fed (the foreign repo pool), and can borrow against their Treasuries through the FIMA repo facility instead of selling them. Japan is the largest foreign holder (, down in August). With the BOJ at and 10-year JGBs at about , Japanese investors earn more at home than they have in decades. A shift in that carry can move US yields. No free daily series for the cross-currency basis was found, so it isn't charted.

    Methodology

    • Debt ticker. Last official Debt to the Penny figure plus the trailing three-month average daily change, extrapolated to now. It is an estimate: the real number moves in steps on settlement days. It stops if the official figure is more than 45 days old.
    • Composition and average rates. MSPD Table I and Treasury's Average Interest Rates, both as of the month-end shown.
    • Maturity wall. Every marketable security in MSPD Table III (one row per CUSIP), summed by maturity month. Bills are shown at face value, TIPS including inflation compensation. The average issued rate uses coupons for notes and bonds, issued-amount-weighted auction yields for bills, and today's 3-month rate plus the fixed spread for FRNs. Bills and FRNs sold after the snapshot date are not included.
    • Refinancing simulator. Rolls the MSPD schedule forward month by month for ten years. Maturing notes and bonds are replaced at the chosen long rate in Treasury's current auction mix; bills are held at the chosen share of debt, with a third of the bill stock repricing each month. Optional borrowing adds the FY2026 primary deficit plus interest. TIPS are counted at their real coupon and FRNs as bills. A sketch of the mechanics, not a forecast.
    • Flow map and scenarios. Starting state is the Fed's H.4.1 balance sheet for Wednesday Oct 28, 2026. Holder buckets come from Z.1 (Q2 2026), H.8, TIC and other sources, each dated in its panel. Scenario amounts are round, illustrative sizes. After every step the page and the test suite check that Fed assets equal liabilities, that bank reserves and deposits move together, and that nothing goes negative.
    • Who owns it. Base is gross debt on Sep 30, 2026. Rows come from MSPD, H.4.1, TIC (Aug 31) and Z.1 (Jun 30). The gap between their sum and the total is shown as "other & unallocated" rather than spread across rows.
    • Net liquidity. A trader heuristic: Fed total assets − TGA − ON RRP, each Wednesday. Not an official measure; shown with its caveats.
    • Rates. Policy rates from FOMC implementation notes and FRED. The standing repo rate equals the top of the target range. SOFR, its percentiles and volumes come from the NY Fed; Treasury yields from the par yield curve via FRED.
    • Refreshing. The scripts in scripts/ re-pull every series (refresh.mjs, build-maturities-mspd.mjs, fetch-history.mjs, fetch-rates.mjs). verify-figures.mjs re-checks figures against their sources, and sync-data.mjs copies the data into this page. Open questions are listed as TODO-verify in docs/mechanisms.md.
    • Position. The page explains mechanics. Where economists disagree (debt sustainability, fiscal dominance, what "liquidity" does to asset prices), it shows the competing views and takes no side.
